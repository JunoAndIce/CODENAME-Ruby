1Overview

Ruby is a mercenary armed with a revolver and a chain whip. She can't swap them for other weapons. The revolver holds six bullets and can't be reloaded until she reaches a new floor, so most kills come from the chain whip.

The whip grabs almost anything in a level, from crates and tables to enemies and their corpses. Ruby can drag what she grabs, reel it in, spin it around herself and throw it. Anything moving fast enough hurts whatever it hits, so a crate, a body or a spinning enemy is a weapon.

Design pillars

  • Snappy controls. Every action responds on the press. Ruby never loses control of her movement to her own abilities: grabbing, pulling and throwing never move her.
  • Weight matters. Every object has a real weight in kilograms. Weight decides how fast Ruby drags, reels and throws an object, and how hard it hits.
  • One damage rule. Every hit in the game uses the same formula, built from speed and weight.
  • Fast deaths. Enemies have little health. A hit fast enough to knock someone down usually kills them.

4Ruby

Ruby weighs 60 kg, as much as an Immovable object, and has 30 health. She walks at 15 m/s and always faces the cursor, so she can move one way while aiming another.

  • Movement and shooting are always on. Ruby can walk and fire while grabbing, pulling, spinning or charging a throw.
  • Nothing she does moves her. The chain never pulls or drags her. When a held object can't follow her, the chain stops her walk instead.
  • She feels weight when she walks into things. She pushes an object at her drag fraction (see Drag fraction), so light crates slide away, heavy ones barely move, and anything 60 kg or heavier blocks her.
  • She can be knocked down. Any hit at Knockdown Speed or faster ragdolls her: her whole body goes limp and tumbles, and she loses control. Once every part of her has come to rest, she gets up where she lies.
  • She comes back when she dies (an early revive, until a proper death and restart exist). She lies where she fell for 2 s (Revive Delay), then stands at her Revive Point, or where she started the level if none is set, with full health and the bullets she started with. The camera jumps there with her. The level is left as it is: dead officers stay dead, and those hunting her lose sight of her and search.
  • Bullets push her. An officer's shot shoves her away from the shooter. A shot she survives pushes her back about 4 m/s, fading within a fifth of a second, and she keeps full control; a wall or the chain stops the push like a step. A killing shot lands the push on the part of her body it hit, so a chest shot knocks her over backward and a shoulder shot twists her as she falls. Today every pistol shot kills her, so only the second case shows.

Ruby's and the officers' ragdolls

Ruby falls on her model's own skeleton, not a capsule, and so do officers. Standing, she's a slim capsule driven by her animation. Knocked down, her limbs take over, and the capsule follows her hips, so the camera and officers keep track of her. The limbs share her 60 kg in fixed proportions from Unity's Ragdoll Wizard (the hips heaviest, the arms and head lightest), so whatever meets her body meets one limb at a time, at that limb's weight. An officer walking into her shoves her limbs aside instead of being blocked.

  • Any humanoid model works. Import it with a Humanoid rig and Optimize Game Objects off, run Unity's Ragdoll Wizard on the model prefab, and give each bone an Impact Damage. SkeletonRagdollBody, on Ruby's root beside her capsule, finds the rest itself; there's nothing to wire.
  • Her limbs take hits. While she's down, each limb is hurt by impacts like any body, at its own weight and once per contact, so a crate striking two limbs hits her twice. Her own limbs striking each other as she falls don't count.
  • The wizard strips colliders. Unity's Ragdoll Wizard deletes every collider, Rigidbody and joint under the bones it sets up, so a gun held in a hand loses its collider and later falls through the floor. After running it, restore the gun's collider.
  • Officers fall limp when thrown, knocked down or killed, the same way. On the chain, though, an officer is tied upright in one piece (see Grabbed).

How Ruby turns

Ruby's body, shots and whip turn to the cursor the instant it moves; only her pose follows a moment behind, to read naturally.

  • Standing still, her head and chest twist toward where she aims. Once that's more than 60° from her feet, her chest pulls her legs round with it, and her feet step round the rest of the way. Running, her legs swing to face where she aims, and she strafes or backpedals when she moves another way.
  • Her left arm points her gun straight out where she aims, level, however close or far the cursor is. It swings round a moment after her aim, but the instant she fires it snaps onto the cursor, so every bullet leaves the barrel and flies along it. Her left hand is closed around the gun.
  • She never looks down. Her pose aims along a level heading, never at the floor under the cursor, and it holds steady while the cursor is within 1.5 m of her, so small mouse moves close to her don't set her twisting.
  • Turning right round, she turns the way her gun hand leads (left), so her arm sweeps round in front of her instead of crossing her body.

Drag fraction

Every weight rule in the game comes from one number: the share of Ruby's strength left over after an object's weight.

Drag fraction
Drag Fraction= clamp( 1−Object WeightPusher's Weight ,0,1)

For the chain, the pusher is always Ruby. When anyone walks into an object, the pusher is the walker, so two characters of equal weight block each other. The fraction is 0 for walls, Immovable objects and anything as heavy as the pusher.

To change how heavy something feels, give it a different weight class, or retune the class in the WeightTable asset. Don't edit Rigidbody masses directly: the Weight component overwrites them at start.

5Ruby's abilities

Chain whip

The whip launches at a node, a grab point placed on the world, a prop, an enemy or a corpse. Once it lands, the chain holds that node until Ruby lets go, throws, or the chain is pulled past its breaking strength.

Grabbing

  • Right click launches the whip at the node marked by the white aim orb.
  • The orb marks the free node nearest the cursor, within 3 m of it (Snap Radius) and within 7 m of Ruby. Clicking far from every node grabs nothing.
  • The whip can't grab through walls. Only walls and Immovable objects block it. Crates and enemies don't, though the chain catches on them once attached.
  • The chain wraps around corners. Ruby's limit is measured along the wrapped chain.

Walking on the chain

Walking toward the node gives the chain slack. Walking away tightens it until it reaches its limit: the current length while Pull is held, or the full 7 m when it isn't. At the limit Ruby drags the object behind her at her drag fraction. If the object can't follow, because it's fixed, wedged against a wall or too heavy, she stops.

Ruby's speed away from the node
Speed Away= max( 0, Chain Limit−DistancePhysics Step+Drag Fraction×Walking Speed )

The first term is the slack left in the chain. When the object can't follow, the chain stays stretched past its limit, that term goes negative and cancels the second, and Ruby stops within a few centimetres. Walking sideways around the node is never limited.

Pulling

Space or Q pulls. A tap tugs the object in once; holding reels it in. Pull does nothing on walls, Immovable objects or anything as heavy as Ruby, so it never drags her toward the node.

Reel-in speed
Reel Speed= min( Pull Impulse, 20 m/s×Drag Fraction )

The square root makes reeling gentler on heavy objects than dragging is. A 50 kg officer, with a drag fraction of 0.17, is reeled in at about 8 m/s but dragged at only 2.5 m/s.

Throwing

While holding an object, hold right click to charge a throw. Left click throws it along Ruby's aim, the line from her through the cursor, harder the longer it charged. The held object jiggles while charging, faster once fully charged.

  • Releasing right click without throwing cancels the charge and keeps hold.
  • A quick tap of right click (under 0.3 s) drops the object instead.
  • Right click held from the launch starts charging the moment the whip lands, so Ruby can grab and throw in one motion.
  • The throw happens on the click and the chain lets go at once, so Ruby can grab again straight away.
  • A thrown object can't hit Ruby for 0.15 s (Throw Pass Through), so something thrown from behind her flies through her.
Charge
Charge= clamp ( Time Held−Tap Threshold Full Charge Time−Tap Threshold ,0,1 )
Throw speed
Throw Speed= Throw Impulse×Charge Multiplier× Ruby's WeightRuby's Weight+Object Weight Charge Multiplier= Min Throw Fraction+(1−Min Throw Fraction)×Charge

The throw replaces whatever speed the object had, so the same cursor position always sends it the same way, wherever it is in a swing.

Spinning

Holding Pull reels the object all the way in to the minimum length (1 m, or more for a big object so it clears Ruby). If it weighs 60 kg or less, it then spins around her. Ruby is the pivot: she keeps walking freely and carries the spin with her, and the spinning object passes through her.

Spin rev-up, every physics step
New Spin= Current Spin+(Spin Speed−Current Spin)×Rev Share Rev Share= 1−e−3×Physics Step÷Spin Up Time

The spin closes about 3% of the gap to Spin Speed every step and reaches 95% of 32 m/s after one second, like an arm winding up. At full speed on a 1 m chain, the object goes round about 5.1 times a second. Spin speed doesn't depend on weight, and the spin ignores where Ruby aims.

Holding the spin on its circle
Extra Pull Each Step= Current Spin2Chain Length×Physics Step

While spinning, the chain's correction limit is raised by exactly this much, so a fast spin never stretches loose. At 32 m/s on a 1 m chain that's 10.2 m/s, on top of the normal 20 m/s limit.

Bouncing off walls
Expected Speed= Current Spin1+(Current Spin×Physics Step÷Chain Length)2 Spin After Bounce= −Spin Bounce×Current Spin

A spinning object bounces when, in one step, it keeps less than half of its expected speed and touches something wall-like: a wall, the side of a crate or an enemy. Scraping the floor doesn't count. It reverses direction, keeps 70% of its speed and revs back up. A swung enemy that hits a wall at Knockdown Speed or faster takes impact damage.

Letting go of a spin

A right-click tap while spinning lets go. The object keeps its speed but is turned onto Ruby's aim, so it flies where she points. A spun officer flies out limp, tumbling (see Ragdoll). At 32 m/s it's well above Knockdown Speed and hurts whatever it hits.

Revolver

For now Ruby carries the officers' pistol model in her left hand. Pulling the trigger snaps her gun arm onto the cursor, and the shot leaves the muzzle in that same frame and flies flat to the cursor: a bullet always comes out of the barrel and goes where she aims, however fast the cursor moved. There's no delay. The gun stays in her hand when she's knocked down.

Semi-automatic: one shot per pull of the trigger, and holding it does nothing. It holds six bullets (Bullet Count) and stops firing when they run out. The revolver fires at all times, including while the chain is holding something. The one exception is charging a throw: while right click is held, left click throws instead of firing.

6Impact damage

Every hit in the game uses one formula: a thrown crate, an enemy swung into a wall, a flung enemy hitting another, or anything fast hitting Ruby. Both bodies in a hit take damage. Walls and props have no health, so they only deal it. A hit counts once, when the contact begins.

Nothing on Ruby's chain hurts her. While she holds something, it can bump her but never damages or knocks her down, and she doesn't hurt it either: reeled in fast, or slipping out of her spin into her, it would otherwise hit hard enough. Once the chain lets go, it's an ordinary object again.

Impact speed
Impact Speed= |Closing Velocity·Hit Direction|

Only the part of the speed heading straight into the hit counts. A head-on hit counts fully; a glancing one counts for less.

Damage
Damage=Damage Scale×Impact Speed×Hitter WeightReference Mass Damage=0if Impact Speed is below Knockdown Speed

A hit at Knockdown Speed or faster also knocks the body down. A knocked-down enemy ragdolls and gets back up into Aggro if it survives. An enemy held on the chain stays held, so a swung enemy keeps swinging while it takes damage. Damage is large from the threshold on purpose: anything fast enough to knock a character down should usually kill.

Tuning rule: keep Knockdown Speed above Ruby's walk plus an officer's chase, or characters running into each other head-on can knock each other down. With the officer's Chase Speed now 13 m/s, that sum is 15 + 13 = 28 m/s, above the current Knockdown Speed of 25: either raise Knockdown Speed to at least 28 or bring Chase Speed back to 10. Spin Speed must stay above Knockdown Speed for swings to hurt.

A tethered object switches to continuous collision detection, because a thrown crate can move almost a metre per physics step and would otherwise pass straight through an enemy.

7Path Grid, colliders and navigation

Enemies find their way over a grid that we bake ourselves. This section is for someone new to the code. It shows how to set a floor up, why a collider decides where an enemy can walk, and which script to open when one won't move. It covers how an enemy gets to a place, not how it picks the place.

The big picture

We don't use Unity's NavMesh. The code lives in its own assembly, Ruby.Pathing (Assets/Scripts/Pathing/), which knows nothing about the game. An enemy plugs in by implementing one small interface, IPathAgent.

  • The floor is a grid of square cells (Cell Size, 0.5 m by default). Each cell is open or blocked. Everything happens on the X and Z axes. Height only decides which floor a point belongs to.
  • One PathGrid per floor. It bakes once, when Play starts, from the colliders in the scene.
  • Two ways to find a route. Which one depends on who wants the goal:
Who wants the goalToolWhy
Many enemies, one goal (chasing Ruby)FlowField (Dijkstra)One flood from the goal gives every cell its distance, and each enemy just steps downhill. The chase field is shared per floor, so chase cost does not grow with the number of enemies.
One enemy, its own goal (walking a patrol route)GridPathfinder (A*)The search heads for the goal and stops there, and the route is cached. A flood of the whole floor would be wasted on a goal nobody else shares.

It is a rule of thumb, not a wall. An enemy walking to a single spot, such as back to their post, floods a field of its own, because it follows the field step by step as it goes.

Setting up a floor

  1. Add a PathGrid to an empty GameObject and put it at the floor's surface. The object's Y is the floor. The grid is a box around its position, lined up with X and Z, Size metres across. Its white outline shows in the Scene view.
  2. Set Size to cover the whole floor. Cells outside it don't exist, and an enemy standing off the grid can't plan.
  3. Set Height (the floor's band). A point belongs to this grid when it lies over it, at most Height above the grid's Y and at most 0.5 m below it. An enemy's root sits about 1 m up (a 2 m capsule centred on it), so Height must be well above that. Stacked floors need a grid each, and their bands must not overlap.
  4. Set Agent Radius to the enemy capsule's radius (0.35 m on the officer prefab) and Agent Height to its height (2 m). The grid decides what fits by pretending to be that capsule. If the two disagree, the grid promises gaps the body can't pass, or refuses ones it could.
  5. Press Play, then select the grid to see what it baked (see Debugging tools below).
SettingDefaultWhat it does

These are the defaults in code. Every one is tunable, and the value on the grid in the scene wins. The test floor's grid, for one, uses an Agent Radius of 0.35 and a Max Flow Distance of 90. Read your own grid's Inspector.

  • Doorways. A gap is open only if some cell centre in it is at least Agent Radius from both sides, and cell centres sit one Cell Size apart. So a doorway needs about 2 × Agent Radius + one Cell Size to be sure of a column of open cells: 1.5 m with the default radius. We build doorways 1.5 m or wider.
  • The grid bakes once, at Start. Change a setting, or move a wall, with Play mode stopped. Corner Margin shows why: it is turned into whole cells at the bake, so an edit made in Play does nothing until the next play. The grid says so, with a warning like Corner Margin 0.75 m is 2 cells but this grid baked 1. The new margin takes effect at the next bake (next play).

Colliders and the grid

How a cell becomes blocked

For every cell the bake stands an invisible capsule on the cell's centre, Agent Radius wide and Agent Height tall, and asks Unity's physics what it overlaps. If it overlaps anything static, the cell is blocked. That is the whole test (PathGrid.IsOpenSpot).

The colours match the Scene view overlay: red is blocked, yellow is open inside the margin.

  • Why this gives walls clearance. The capsule is the enemy's body. A cell is blocked when their body would touch something standing on it, so the open cells are exactly the places their centre can be. Paths never need extra room for their width. The margin on top is covered under Navigation below.
  • Why the floor doesn't count. The capsule's underside floats Floor Clearance (0.1 m) above the grid's Y, and its top is Agent Height up. So the grid's Y must be at the floor's top surface. Put the grid below the floor and the floor overlaps every capsule, so the whole grid comes out blocked.
  • Only the capsule's band counts. A collider wholly below 0.1 m (a flat rug) or above 2 m (a ceiling) blocks nothing. A low beam under 2 m does.
  • Triggers never block, and layers never matter. The test ignores trigger colliders and looks at every layer. So an invisible wall needs a solid collider, on any layer.

Static and dynamic bodies

The bake asks one question of each collider it touches: is it fixed? A collider is fixed when the Rigidbody it belongs to is kinematic, or when it has no Rigidbody above it at all. Unity's own Static checkbox plays no part.

The colliderThe bakeExamples
No Rigidbody on it or any parentBlocks, for the whole play sessionWalls, built-in furniture
On a kinematic RigidbodyBlocks, same as aboveAn Immovable prop
On a dynamic RigidbodySkippedCrates, loose guns, enemies, Ruby
A triggerIgnoredA corpse at rest (its colliders are triggers)

A child collider belongs to the nearest Rigidbody above it, so a collider on a crate's mesh child is part of the crate. Moving things are skipped because the grid is baked once: a crate baked in would leave a wall behind when it moved, and enemies would wall each other off. Things that move but should still block are the job of GridObstacle.

GridObstacle: a prop that blocks while it rests

The bake skips a dynamic crate, so without help enemies plan straight through it. Put GridObstacle on any prop that should block paths. It needs a Rigidbody, and it works like this:

  • At rest it marks the grid. Once the prop has stayed under Rest Speed (0.2 m/s, with spin counted as the speed its corners sweep at) for Settle Time (0.01 s), it blocks every cell its solid colliders would touch with an enemy capsule, the same clearance the bake gives a wall. Cells it blocks show orange in the Scene view.
  • Moving, it clears. A shoved or thrown prop gives its cells back, so no stale cells hang in the air behind it. It re-marks once it settles. A slow creep that shifts its footprint by half a cell is re-marked too.
  • It costs nothing while resting. It only touches the grid when it settles or starts to move.
  • Every change tells the grid. Each mark or clear bumps the grid's Version, and flow fields and cached routes re-plan within about 0.2 s.

No GridObstacle, no blocking. Enemies plan through a prop that lacks one, then meet it as an ordinary physics body. One more catch: a GridObstacle whose prop rests outside every grid can't block anything, and it logs a warning once to say so.

Immovable objects

Giving a body the Weight class Immovable makes its Rigidbody kinematic (Weight owns that flag). That happens in Awake, before the bake at Start, so the bake sees it as fixed and blocks its cells with no GridObstacle. Adding one to it would add nothing. Because the bake is a one-off, an Immovable prop moved later by script keeps its old cells blocked and doesn't block its new spot.

Characters collide with their root capsule only

Visual children (the model, the gun, the eyes) carry no colliders. For pathing this matters because the grid assumes the enemy is a capsule of Agent Radius. A model collider that sticks out makes the body wider than the grid believes, so it snags corners the grid called clear. A collider left on a decorative mesh with no Rigidbody becomes a wall in the bake, wherever it happens to stand. The one exception is a ragdoll: its bones' colliders take over from the capsule while it's limp, and they sit on dynamic Rigidbodies, so the bake skips them.

My enemy won't walk there

Start with the Console. The grid and navigator log a warning that names the enemy (click it to select them). It says so once per message, so a goal that keeps moving can repeat it. The text below is exact, with the enemy's name in front.

Navigation

Who does what

GridNavigator plans, and the agent steers. The navigator never moves a body. Each physics step it works out a point to head for and hands it to the agent through IPathAgent: MoveToward(target, speed, mustReach) and Stop(). The agent also reports its Body (where planning starts) and its StuckTime. EnemyController implements all of this, turning its heading and writing its own velocity. The split keeps Ruby.Pathing free of game rules, and lets each kind of character keep its own movement.

Three navigator calls cover what enemies do: a patrol or a search walks to an area (MoveToArea, A*), a chase follows the shared field toward its target (ChaseTo), and a walk back to their post goes to a point on a field of its own (MoveTo). Each step they all do the same three things:

  1. Arrived? Within 0.5 m of the goal, or standing in the goal's cell: stop.
  2. Is there a clear straight line? If so, head straight for the goal and skip the search.
  3. Otherwise follow the route. That is the flow field's next step, or the next corner of the A* route.

A patrol stop is an area (GridArea: a circle, or a rectangle lined up with X and Z), not a point. They head for its centre, but if the centre is blocked (a table, an anchor) the nearest open cell inside the area counts. An exact point on an object could never be reached.

The flow field and A*

  • Flow field. A Dijkstra flood outward from the goal: a straight step costs 10, a diagonal 14. Each enemy reads its neighbours and steps toward the lowest cost. The flood stops at Max Flow Distance, measured in plain path metres (wall costs don't count), so wall costs can never shorten a chase's reach. A cell the flood never reached is off the field, and that gives the no path to ... beyond Max Flow Distance warning. A chase field re-floods when its target changes cell, and any field re-floods when the grid changes, throttled to once per 0.2 s.
  • A*. Searches from the enemy to the goal cell with the same costs, and ties go to the cell nearer the goal, so open rooms get a straight run. It gives up after 20,000 cells (the "too far for one search" warning) so a hopeless search can't hitch a frame. The route is cached. It's re-planned when the grid changes, or when the enemy is knocked off the route, meaning the next corner is out of their sight.
  • No corner cutting. Both only take a diagonal step when the two cells beside it are open too, so nobody squeezes between two blocked corners.
  • Hugging a wall. An enemy touching a wall often stands in a cell the margin marks blocked. Planning starts from the nearest open cell within two cells of them, and their first cell is exempt from sight checks.

Keeping off corners

The grid keeps a second number for each cell, its clearance: how many cells it is from the nearest blocked cell or the grid's edge, capped just past the margin. Corner Margin is turned into whole cells (rounded up, so never less than asked). A cell at or under that clearance is inside the margin, and stepping onto it adds a cost: (margin cells − clearance + 1) × Wall Cost. With the defaults a step onto a cell beside a wall adds 20, the price of two straight steps.

  • It is a price, not a wall. Paths keep half a metre off corners where there is room and run down the middle of a doorway instead of hugging a jamb. A gap too narrow for the margin is still walked, down its middle, because that is the cheapest line through. A margin never closes a route.
  • Straight lines keep the margin too. The "clear straight line" test above needs every cell along it to be outside the margin, so a shortcut can't graze a corner that the flood routed round. A goal beside a wall can still be walked straight to: the test relaxes only near the goal, as a ramp. A line that fails goes to the field or A*.

String pulling and line of sight

A route over cells is a staircase of eight-direction steps. Walked as it is, enemies would zigzag. So the route is pulled tight: from each point, look as far ahead as there is a straight line, and keep a corner only where sight breaks. For A* this is string pulling on the finished path. The flow field does the same with a look-ahead of 8 cells, aiming at the furthest one in sight.

  • Line of sight is exact. It walks the cells the line crosses rather than sampling points, so a line can't slip through the corner of a blocked cell.
  • The pull keeps the margin it had room for. Where the path had room, the pulled line keeps the full margin. Through a narrow gap it keeps only the clearance the gap has, which is its middle. This is a rule, not a fallback.
  • They turn on sight, not on arrival. An enemy on a route moves on to the next corner as soon as that corner is in straight sight, so they start their bend early instead of walking onto the corner first. The same sight test, at the same clearance, tells them when they have been knocked off.

Steering: why bends are arcs

The navigator names a point and EnemyController.MoveToward does the steering, using Steering, which is pure maths. It turns their walking heading at Turn Speed (720 degrees a second by default) rather than pivoting to face the point, so a bend is an arc that starts before the corner. A standing body with no direction to turn to keeps its facing.

  • The danger is orbiting. A body turning at a fixed rate at speed v sweeps a circle of radius v divided by the turn rate. A point inside that circle can never be reached: they circle it for ever.
  • SpeedToFit slows them just enough that the arc passes through the point. Within 30 degrees of straight ahead nothing is slowed, and they never drop below a tenth of their speed, so a point right behind them can't freeze them.
  • Only for points they must reach. That means a goal on a clear line, a route corner, or a field's last step into the goal cell (the mustReach flag). Any other field step rolls ahead of them every step and can't be orbited, so slowing there would only slow a chase on ordinary bends.
  • Turn Speed is tunable. Lower it for wider arcs. The legs' own turn on the model (CharacterAnimator) is a separate setting.

When are they stuck?

An area move (a patrol or a search) has a watchdog. If the distance left along the route doesn't shrink by 0.25 m for Stuck Time (1.5 s by default), they are blocked: if they are already inside the area it counts as arrived, and otherwise the move reports Blocked with the reason you saw in the table. Without it a walker would push at a jammed doorway for ever. Chase and single-spot walks have no watchdog. They keep stepping down their field.

No silent fallbacks

When the navigator can't plan a move, it does not quietly do something else. It stops the body and says why. Chase and point walks log a warning (Fail) and stand still. The same message repeats only after a successful move, so a stuck enemy doesn't flood the Console, but a goal that keeps moving changes the message and logs again. Area moves (a patrol or a search) return Blocked or Unreachable with a FailureReason, and the caller decides: the patrol skips the point with a warning. They never walk straight at the goal through a wall, and never wanders off.

Two behaviours look like fallbacks but are written rules: a path lowers its clearance to what a narrow gap allows, and a patrol move that stalls inside its area counts as arrived. Neither hides a failure.

8Enemies

Police officer

The demo's only enemy. An officer weighs 50 kg, has 20 health and carries a pistol in their right hand. Officers are a squad defending their building: they see, hear and remember, share what they know a little late, check disturbances together and fight as one group. The model is Hitman's AI: each officer has their own knowledge and reacts to what they sense, and the squad shares a picture of where Ruby is.

Values below are as tuned on the officer prefab and the test scene's Intel object.

How they walk. An officer's route keeps half a metre off walls and corners and runs down the middle of a doorway, so they never scrape a jamb. Where the way bends they turn in an arc, starting before the corner, instead of stopping to pivot, and they turn on the spot before setting off. They keep 2 m of personal space from other officers. They slow only to reach a point they must reach, never mid-route.

Props on the floor. Patrolling and searching officers walk round props. Hunting or rearming officers shove light props (under 20 kg) out of the way and go round heavier ones, giving up a shove when going round is cheaper or the prop jams. They fit through any gap about 0.75 m wide or wider, and they don't drag crates by rubbing against them.

Pistol

  • One shot kills Ruby. A bullet does 30 damage against her 30 health, and knocks her body away from the shooter (see Ruby). Bullets are visible and fly at 40 m/s, under three times her walking speed, so a sidestep can still dodge one from mid-range. Officers take no push from bullets, so a held shield doesn't budge.
  • Fires on the move. An armed officer fires every 0.6 s whenever Ruby is in sight within 15 m and on her feet, after the Draw beat on first sight. Once she's down, officers lower their guns.
  • Keeps its distance. With Ruby in sight, an armed officer walks to a firing spot of their own and holds there. With no spot, they close in only to 5 m and hold between 5 and 6 m. If she comes closer they back away, still facing her and firing. Cornered, they hold their ground. Ruby walks faster than they back off, so she can still run a gunman down.
  • What stops a bullet: walls, crates and props, Ruby, and anything Ruby holds on the chain. A held officer or corpse is a shield. Free officers and resting corpses let bullets through, so there's no friendly fire.
  • Drops. An officer drops their pistol when knocked down and when killed; the gun keeps the officer's momentum and skids, so a knockdown sends it flying. When the chain grabs them, it pops out of their hand with a small hop instead. It never starts inside or passes through a wall.
  • A dropped pistol is a prop. The chain can grab, reel and throw it, but never take one out of a hand. Ruby can't pick one up or fire it.

What an officer sees, hears and knows

Sight

  • Officers see in a straight line from eye height. Walls, crates and tables block their sight; other characters don't.
  • Everything is seen through the cone. An officer's view is an 80° cone in front of them out to 15 m. Ruby, corpses, loose guns and moving bodies are all noticed only inside it.
  • A close circle, for Ruby only. Within 2.5 m all round they notice Ruby even behind them, so she can't stand at their back unnoticed.
  • In a fight they see further. Once hunting her, an officer keeps track of Ruby at any distance they have a clear line to her.
  • Windows are Planned.

Hearing

  • Sounds travel along the floor. A gunshot carries 20 m, the whip landing 5 m, and an impact 10 m, growing with how hard the hit was up to 20 m. It's measured along the walkable floor, so it doesn't carry through a wall, or to another floor.
  • A thrown body leads back to Ruby. Anything she holds on the chain remembers where she stood, for 5 seconds after she lets go, and passes that on to whatever it hits. An officer who sees it fly, or hears it hit, goes to where it was thrown from, not where it landed.
  • Ruby's own sounds teach them where she is. A gunshot or the whip tells everyone who hears it where she stood.

What a disturbance is worth

Each kind of thing an officer senses has a priority from 1 to 5 and a response: an attack to search for, or a problem to check. These come from a behaviour map, and each room can have its own (see Rooms), so a gunshot in the firing range or a loose gun in the armory can be ignored. The default map:

DisturbancePriorityResponse

  • A thud can be an attack. A problem is searched for as an attack instead when the officer knows Ruby is about, or when the newest news of her is under 15 seconds old and within 20 m of it.
  • Moving bodies. Only a body on the chain or tumbling counts, never an officer standing or walking. Each one is reacted to once every 5 seconds.
  • Bodies and guns the squad knows about. One the squad has never reported goes on its books and is checked. One it already knows about only gets a glance: the officer faces it and looks for a moment from where they stand, and is wary from then on.
  • What sends someone straight to a fight: seeing Ruby, seeing her drag or throw an officer, seeing a squadmate on a job go down, and being told she's here by someone who sees her.

What they know

  • What they know beats what's true. Each officer remembers the newest thing they know of Ruby: what they saw, heard or were told, or the squad's shared picture, whichever is newer. Out of sight, nobody ever knows her real position.
  • The squad's shared picture updates every 0.7 s (Intel Interval), so it lags a little behind what anyone saw. It also holds the bodies and loose guns the squad has reported.
  • They call out what they see. While they see Ruby, an officer shouts her position at once and then every 2 s. Officers within 9 m along the floor learn it immediately, and so does everyone in the speaker's room when it's a small room. A shout doesn't carry through a wall. Everyone who hears joins the fight.
  • They call out the vanish. When Ruby slips out of sight, the officer says once where she vanished and which way she was heading.
  • They never forget her face. Once an officer has seen Ruby, or been told she's about by someone who has, they know she's in the building, for good. From then on they patrol aggressively.

Working together

Group jobs: checking things as a team

One disturbance becomes one job, and up to 6 officers work it together. A sound or sight within 5 m (Job Match Radius) of a job's places goes to that job instead of starting another, and two jobs about the same place merge. Free officers nearby are called in to fill an open job.

  • A Check is for a problem. The lead reacts first (0.68 s), then calls the others in one at a time (0.8 s apart). One officer, the checker, goes to the spot and looks; the rest follow 5 m behind. Then they stand down together.
  • A Search is for an attack. The team walks to the source first, stopping within 5 m of it, then spreads out to clear the area. The area is every room the floor reaches within 15 m of the source, searched whole out to its walls, plus open ground in no room.
  • Clearing by sight. The area is split into points about a metre apart, and a point is cleared when anyone sees it. Each member walks to the nearest unseen point nobody else has claimed, nearest on foot rather than in a straight line, sweeping their view as they go at a slow creep, and moves on as soon as it's seen. The search ends once every point is seen and at least 7 s (Search Minimum Time) have passed. A point nobody can reach is dropped with a warning.
  • A search grows. Something new near the area adds the rooms round it to the search. A gunshot inside it throws away what was seen, so they look again.
  • Telling. A member who has seen Ruby tells the rest of the job she's about. Being told isn't seeing: only an officer who has seen her can tell others.
  • Distractions on a search. An ordinary disturbance sends one member to check it. A serious one (a gunshot, an unreported body, an officer seen flying) brings the whole job there, keeping its search.
  • The third look is an attack. From the third job an officer is put on, every job they join is a Search for an attack.
  • A job mate going down in their sight sends the others after Ruby.

Fights

  • One fight per floor. Everyone hunting Ruby on a floor is in one fight, all equal. Contact is shared: while anyone in the fight sees her, nobody gives up.
  • Firing spots. Each armed officer with Ruby in sight claims a firing spot of their own round her, at least 2 m from anyone else's, favouring a different angle from their mates'. A fight spreads into a loose crossfire instead of closing along one line.
  • The glimpse grace. Sight flickers at a crate's edge, so Ruby counts as gone only after a quarter second without it. Meanwhile they hold their spot and keep the gun on where she was, without firing.
  • Covering the ways out. Out of sight, the fight finds the ways out of where Ruby vanished: doorways, corridor ends, directions across an open room, out to 8 m of floor. The way she was heading goes to the nearest officer, every other opening gets an officer before any gets a second, and no route takes more than two. Everyone else spreads round the area at least 2 m apart. On arrival they face outward, down their route. A route holder keeps their route when a new call-out moves the plan a little.
  • Losing her. When nobody in the fight has seen Ruby for 5 s (Lose Sight Time), the fight is lost. The officers hold the Lose beat, then start a Search job where they lost her, grown toward where she was heading. Officers past the job's cap patrol aggressively instead.
  • Provoked. An officer on a search who is disturbed 3 times within 15 s (two within 1.5 s count as one) has had enough of Ruby and goes after her, to where the last disturbance came from. They see her only with their own eyes, in their cone, until a hit gives their all-round fighting sight back.

Rooms

  • A room is placed in the scene: one or more rectangles with a name, edited with handles like patrol areas. Rooms can sit inside bigger rooms, and the smallest room holding a point is the one that counts. Overlaps that make that ambiguous are warned about when the scene loads.
  • Each room can have its own behaviour map, so the same thing is ignored in one room and searched for in another. A point in no room uses the level's default map.
  • Searches go by room: a room the search reaches is searched whole. A shout fills the speaker's room, when it's a small one.
  • The firing range. An officer posted at a range spot practises with real shots every 1.2 to 2.5 s, and the range's map stops those shots alarming anyone.

Idle

Holds their post until they see Ruby or are hurt, either of which sends them into Aggro. A disturbance sends them to check it (see What an officer sees, hears and knows). Where they start is their post. After a search, a fall or a shove, they walk back and turn the way they started. If the way back takes more than 20 seconds (another officer standing on the post, say), they stay where they are and try again next time. An idle officer given an Aggressive route walks it once they're aggressive; without one they stay at their post, alert. An officer posted at a firing range practises there (see Rooms).

Patrol

  • Walks the patrol route at 4 m/s, pausing 2.5 s at each stop. A stop can set its own pause. During a long pause (10 s or more) they move about the stop every 3 to 6 s instead of standing frozen.
  • Each stop is an area, not an exact point: a circle, or a rectangle lined up with the grid, so it can fill a room or run along a corridor. The officer heads for its centre, and if the centre is occupied, the nearest open spot inside the area counts as reached.
  • Mode sets the order: Loop (0, 1, 2, 0), Ping Pong (0, 1, 2, 1) or Random. Random picks any stop except the one the officer is at and the one they just came from.
  • A route with one stop 4 m or more across is wandered: the officer walks to random open spots inside it, pausing at each, so a long corridor gets paced along. A smaller single stop means "stand here". The Scene view labels a wandered stop "0 · wander".
  • After being pulled off their route, they rejoin at the nearest stop, not the first.
  • Aggressive. Once an officer knows Ruby is about, they patrol aggressively for good. They walk their Aggressive route if they have one (a second route, with an A before each stop's number in the Scene view) and otherwise keep walking their Safe route, 20% faster, looking around at each pause.
  • A stop they can't reach is skipped with a warning. If every stop fails, they wait 3 s and try again.
  • Routes are edited with handles in the Scene view, on the officer. Drag a circle's rim dot to size it, or a rectangle's edges, as on a Box Collider.

Aggro

  • Entered on seeing Ruby, on taking damage, on being told by a squadmate who sees her, or on getting up after being knocked down. From a calm state it starts with the Draw beat.
  • In sight, they hunt Ruby at 13 m/s. Armed, they walk to a firing spot and fire as they move (see Pistol and Fights).
  • Out of sight, they cover a way out of where she vanished, or spread round the area, and look outward. They know her path, never her position.
  • When nobody in the fight has seen her for 5 s, they hold the Lose beat and start searching. Knowing nothing at all of her ends the hunt at once.

Searching

  • Searches at 3 m/s, after a Notice beat when they were idle or patrolling. Most searches are group jobs (see Working together).
  • A problem gets a quick look: 2 s of looking round, then the Stand Down beat, then back to their post or route.
  • An attack gets a Search job that clears the area room by room. An officer searching alone (a glance, or a scene with no Intel object) looks round for 15 s.
  • A spot they can't reach within 30 s is searched from where they stand.
  • A search gives way only to something worth more. A higher-priority disturbance sends them there instead. A lesser one distracts them: they turn toward it for 1.5 s, then carry on, and it counts toward being provoked (see Fights).

Rearm

  • An officer without a gun goes for the nearest free one within 12.5 m (Rearm Radius): lying still, not on Ruby's chain, and not already claimed by another officer, so two never race for one gun.
  • They run there at chase speed and pick the gun up just before touching it, so they never kick it away. A gun against a wall or in a corner they pick up from as far as 1 m. Then they go back to hunting.
  • If the gun starts moving (Ruby yanks it away), is taken, or can't be reached, they try the next one. With none left they chase Ruby unarmed, and wait 3 s before looking again.

Grabbed

  • Held by Ruby's chain, tied upright in one piece, even if they were already down or dead. Their pistol pops out of their hand, and they can't move on their own: they're dragged, reeled, spun and thrown like any object. While held they stop bullets, so Ruby can use them as a shield.
  • Thrown, they go limp and hit whatever they fly into.
  • Dropped with a quick right-click tap, they fall limp where they are. If Ruby was spinning them, they fly out limp along her aim instead.
  • Officers who see a squadmate grabbed search round them, and go for Ruby when she drags one.

Ragdoll

  • Knocked down, thrown or let go, the officer goes limp and tumbles on their own skeleton. Once they settle, they stand still for Get Up Time (1 s), then hunt in Aggro, with no draw. A hit at Knockdown Speed during the get-up knocks them down again.

Dead

  • A dead officer becomes a corpse, darkened to 35% brightness. Corpses are never removed.
  • An officer killed while held on the chain stays held.
  • Corpses can still be grabbed, swung and thrown, and hit like any other body. They take no further damage.
  • A corpse at rest has no collision: Ruby and officers walk over it. It can still be grabbed.

11The world of Parabellum

For artistic inspiration.

CODENAME: Ruby takes place in the world of Parabellum, the franchise it belongs to.

The story is set almost 300 years after the fall of our current civilization. Three hundred years ago, humans born with elemental powers were discovered, and the world was thrown into chaos. Over the next three centuries the elementalists became all that remained. After countless wars left the earth unrecognizable, they set out to rebuild civilization. The leaders of the war effort became political leaders, and those who followed them laid the groundwork for future generations to thrive. The country we once knew as the United States of America became the United Front.*

The United Front set out to be a refuge for anyone seeking safety from the war: a nation that turns away no one except those who wish to see it fall, and that houses elementalists of every kind. It is also a nation where the roots of corruption had already dug deep.

After its founding, the United Front enjoyed a short era of peace. It set up its systems of government and founded small cities that would grow into great metropolises. Its capital is Central City.

Technology has boomed thanks to brilliant minds across the world. Cybernetics are now legal in three fields:

  • Weaponry
  • Medicine
  • Luxury upgrades for the upper class

The government of Central City and the United Front is not like our own. It has three branches:

  • The Royal Guard
  • The U.F.S.F. (United Front Special Forces)
  • The CCPD (Central City Police Department)

The corruption grew out of what happened during the 300 years of war:

  • Factions formed between the elements. This complicated the war, because some elementalists sided with humans, while others sought to wipe out non-elementalists and anyone who disagreed with them.
  • Once the last humans had either converted or been killed, the elementalist leaders called a truce, but no peace treaty was ever signed. A council of leaders from each major faction met in what was once Berlin, Germany, and chose the leaders who would rebuild civilization. Some of them still longed for battle and held grudges against other factions.

So after roughly 300 years, some groups of elementalists still want to fight, and still want the power to make the rules.

The Royal Guard has fallen to an internal coup d'état, and these groups are using the chaos to seize power.

  • Since the fall of the Royal Guard, corrupt police factions have ruled Central City.
  • Ruby's parents were both police officers. Ruby discovered that both of them aided and abetted corrupt officers, and that only her father truly tried to do the right thing. Her mother was Deputy Chief of Police, and her superiors used her to commit crimes.
  • When Ruby found out, she couldn't do anything about it.
  • A rival police squad, which served the wealthy districts, opposed Captain Rector, captain of the Lower Slums squad. That squad was led by three women: Gensha, Omara and Zahra.
  • Ruby's partner, Taka, was brutally assaulted by the squad her father once belonged to. That squad is now run by his old rival, who abuses his power wherever he can to run his own small empire in the lower slums.
  • Years later, Ruby returns to that squad to give them what they're owed.

* Working name. "The United Front" is not final.
